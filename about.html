Community Reach documentation

Who in the WordPress community has gone quiet, and who active is near enough to reach them. This page explains what the tool shows, where every number comes from, and what it cannot see.

What this is

Every member of the two Make WordPress community Slack channels, joined to their WordPress.org profile, placed on a map, and labelled with how recently they have been visible. Select anyone and the panel shows the closest active people to them.

The point is proximity. A dormant supporter with an active one an hour away is a specific, actionable lead in a way that a list of names is not.

This is a re-engagement tool, not a scoreboard. It exists to find people worth checking in on. It is private, it names individuals, and it must not be shared outside the team or published anywhere public.

Using it

The layout

The list on the left, the map on the right, and both show the same thing: whatever you have switched on. Picking someone opens their record where the list was, and the map moves to them. Nothing else changes and nothing switches mode on you.

Every filter is additive, and nothing is hidden by default. Statuses are chips you turn on in any combination — slowing and active is a view you can ask for. Turning them all off means all of them, not none. People, Meetups and Automatticians are three switches in Show, and each one that is on contributes a section to the list and its own markers to the map. The chips under Show belong to whichever layers are on.

The expand control on the map — the corner arrows, under the zoom buttons — hides the list and gives the map the whole width. It is a view, not a mode: only that control turns it on or off, and selecting someone leaves it alone.

The preset, and how the list is ordered

Gone quiet and Reachable now in the masthead are not only counters. Clicking either switches on a preset — a saved selection you can turn off like anything else. The tool opens with Gone quiet on, so it lands on the job it exists for rather than on several thousand undifferentiated rows.

While a preset is on, the list is ranked, not sorted by how long someone has been quiet. People with an active person within reach come first, because those are the ones you can act on today. Anyone with nobody in range sits at the bottom: that is a coverage gap, not a lead. With the preset off, the list is in last-seen order, most recent first — the only order that means anything across a mixed selection of statuses. Whichever is in force is printed above the list.

Picking a status chip clears the preset, and switching a preset on clears the status chips. They are two ways of asking the same question, and combining them would mostly return nothing while looking as though it should not.

  • ↑ and ↓ move through the people without going back to the list. They walk people only, never meetups.
  • Esc closes a record. The list returns exactly where you left it.
  • Show more extends a section sixty at a time. Each section has its own.

The controls

There is no population switch. Everyone in the two Slack channels is always shown. People holding a Community Team role are labelled rather than filtered to: a badge beside their name in the list and their record, a larger ringed dot on the map, the Role column in the table, and their role is searchable. A control that swapped the whole population meant the same link could show 96 people or 7,032 depending on a button two clicks away, and it sometimes opened on the wrong one.
ControlWhat it does

Layers

Shape is the layer, colour is the status, so the two can be read independently and neither depends on telling colours apart. Turning a layer on gives it its own chips and its own section in the list.

Automatticians are the one exception to Place: automattic.com/map publishes coordinates and no country, so they cannot be filtered by it without inventing one. The section says so rather than quietly showing the wrong 1,346 people.

The key is on the map itself, behind the button under the zoom controls.

Sending someone a view

The address bar tracks what you are looking at, so copy it and paste it. View, place, state, every status chip, every layer and its chips, the preset, the search and the person you have open all travel — “the US, slowing and active people, active meetups” is a link. Whoever opens it enters the passphrase and lands exactly where you were.

A link carries a name, never a judgement. The only thing about a person that travels in a link is their WordPress.org username, which is already public on their profile. Their status, last-seen date, employer and location never do. This tool exists to re-engage people, not to circulate a list of who has gone quiet.

Working set, and the map

Marking people, meetups and Automatticians collects them in the working set, which survives filtering and reload, and exports to CSV. It is your own scratch list; it is not shared, and it does not travel in a link.

Reset view, the circular arrow under the zoom controls, returns the map to the frame your current filters would give it — for when you have flown in on someone and changed your mind. It resets the view only: whoever you have open stays open.

The data, and every number in it

7,032 people, 2,858 of them placed on the map, current to 2026-08-26. This section is the answer to the questions that get asked out loud while the tool is on screen.

The two headline numbers are nested, not side by side

This is the first thing anyone asks. They are not meant to sum to 7,032: the second is a slice of the first. Each carries its denominator on screen for exactly that reason.

Both are measured against the population — place, state and search — and never against the status chips. Pressing one of them therefore does not move it, and narrowing to Dormant does not empty the pool of active neighbours the reachability is measured from.

The number to be ready for: 2,757 unknown. That is 39% of the dataset, and it is deliberately not counted as gone quiet. We have never seen these people active, so we cannot say they went quiet. They joined a channel and read without posting, or their work happens somewhere none of the three sources below can see. Counting them would take the headline from 3,620 to 6,377 on evidence we do not have. Treat it as no evidence either way, never as gone.

Where a last-seen date comes from

Status is the freshest signal from any of these, not from Slack alone. Someone silent in Slack for two years while filing pull requests every week reads as active, and the record says which source saw them.

What it cannot see

  • 4,174 people have no dot. Their .org profile has no location. They are still counted and still in the table. Nothing is ever placed on a guess.
  • A meetup's window is much wider than a person's. A group counts as Meeting if it met within 365 days; a person counts as active if a source saw them within 30. That is correct — one meeting a quarter is a healthy group and a silent quarter is not a healthy person — but it used to mean 130 groups were labelled “Active” green while a person with the identical gap was labelled “Dormant” red, on the same map. The windows are unchanged, because they belong to the events dashboard and both tools must report the same numbers. The words changed instead: meetups are Meeting, Stopped or Never met, so no word means two things on one screen.
  • Automatticians cannot be filtered by Place. automattic.com/map publishes coordinates and no country, and this tool does not infer one.
  • Work outside these three sources is invisible. Forums, translation, WordCamp organising and mentoring do not reach any of them, so somebody genuinely busy can read as quiet.

"Unknown" is not a synonym for inactive. It means nothing we can read has seen them — not Slack, not Help Scout, not GitHub. That is mostly people who joined the channels and read without posting, plus anyone whose work happens somewhere we cannot see. Treat it as "no evidence either way," never as "gone."

A dot never tells you how precise a location is. Someone who wrote “Germany” sits at the middle of Germany and looks exactly like someone who wrote “Berlin”. The distinction is real and it is recorded, but it is shown in words rather than in the dot: their record and their map popup both say (country only). An earlier version of this page claimed hollow dots carried it. They never did.

Selecting a person zooms to them so you can see who surrounds them. Clicking any of their nearby people selects that person in turn.

How fresh each source is

Sources are pulled on different days, so they are not equally current. This build is dated 2026-08-26, which is the newest thing in it. The per-source dates and counts are in the table under The data, kept in one place so the two cannot disagree.

If one source falls behind the others, someone quiet in it may simply not have been swept since. That is why status is the freshest signal across all of them rather than any single one.

What it cannot see

  • Coverage is uneven between sources. Slack has seen 4,219 people, GitHub 153 and Help Scout 68 — because only 103 people have Help Scout accounts at all, and a GitHub link is only found where someone put one on their profile. An absent signal means we could not look, not that nothing happened.
  • WordCamp and meetup organising. Not yet a source. Someone running events every month can still read as quiet here.
  • Private channels we are not in. Activity there reads as silence.
  • Location, for 4,174 people. They never filled the field in. There is no way to recover this from their profile, only by asking.
  • Ambiguous places. A bare "Georgia" could be the country or the US state. Where it is genuinely unclear, the person is left unplaced rather than dropped on the wrong continent.

The map tells you who to ask. It does not tell you what is going on in someone's life, and it is not evidence of anything on its own.

Correcting something

You will know things the data does not. Corrections beat every automated source.

  1. Select the person and click Suggest a correction. It shows their key and opens the shared Google Sheet.
  2. Find that key in the Sheet's key column.
  3. Fill in SET STATUS or SET LOCATION, put your name in BY, and say what you know in WHY.
A correction with no reason is skipped. Not to be difficult. Six months from now an override with no explanation is indistinguishable from a mistake, and nobody will know whether to trust it. One sentence is plenty.

Corrections are applied on the next rebuild, so allow a day. They are not instant, and nothing you type on the map page itself is saved.

Using it with Claude

The map answers "who is near this person." For anything more specific, take the data to an AI agent. No setup, no connector.

  1. Set the filters you want, or leave them wide. The export follows them.
  2. Switch to Table and click Export. You get a CSV of exactly the rows in view.
  3. Attach that CSV to Claude and ask.

A prompt that works well as a starting point:

This CSV is a snapshot of the WordPress community: one row per person,
with their role, their last-seen date, an activity status, and their
city, country, latitude and longitude.

Find every person whose status is "dormant" or "inactive" who has an
"active" person within 20 miles of them. Use the lat/lng columns to
work out the distance.

Give me a table: the quiet person, their role, how long they have been
quiet, the nearest active person, and the distance. Sort so the most
recently-quiet people come first, because they are the easiest to
bring back.

Swap "20 miles" for whatever radius makes sense, or ask it to group by country, find clusters with no active person at all, or draft an opening message for each pair. The columns are plain and it handles them well.

The export is real people. It carries names and locations, so it belongs in the same places this dashboard does and nowhere else.
